1.0.1 — 2026-11-08

Listening

The mini-player's bar no longer moves your track. The thin line along the bottom of the mini-player shows where the track is, and nothing more: a touch near the bottom edge of your phone — where the phone's own gestures start — no longer sends the music elsewhere in the track. To move within a track, open the full-screen player. A swipe on the mini-player now works from its very bottom too.

The album details hold still. Tapping the cover opens the album's details without the bubble flickering as it opens and closes. Its tracklist names each track once — it repeated an album queued several times —, and an album on two discs reads disc by disc.

Library

The Refresh tells you when it is done. Once a Refresh of your library has read the music folder and the list is back, a message says so: Library refreshed — or Library up to date when the folder has not changed since it was last read, the sign to check that your new albums were copied where the box looks.

Know when a USB drive comes and goes. Plug a USB drive into the box and a message tells you what became of it: the drive that holds your music library is mounted and ready — or the message says why not —; another drive tells you how to make it your library, or that it cannot be used. Pull one out and you are told too. The message arrives as a notification on the devices you subscribed, and in the app while it is open.

Changing the library's drive takes effect. Choosing another USB drive in Guided replaced the library only once the previous drive was pulled out or the box restarted; it now takes effect when you apply — and if the previous drive is still in use, Guided tells you what to close first.

An unplugged library drive no longer holds things up. With the drive that holds your music library pulled out, whatever reads its folder — playback, a screen of the app — gives up within seconds, where it waited a minute and a half.

The queue holds still, and keeps up. A track you remove leaves the list under your finger and the rest stays where it is — no more flash, no more jump back to the top. The button beside the track playing now plays and pauses — its cover opens the full-screen player —, and the queue moves on by itself when a track ends. Outputs and Sources no longer blank either when you switch an output or connect a service.

Arrange the queue, and play any track of it. Hold a track by its handle and slide it where you want it — to the top of the list, it plays next; the list scrolls by itself as you near its edge. Tap a track and it plays. Up next now shows what is really coming: the tracks already played leave the list, and with repeat on, those that will play again follow the last one.

Play a whole album from your media server. On a media server such as MinimServer, albums and playlists now carry Play and +: play one whole, or add it to the queue. Open it and tap a track: the album plays from that track on. Long lists go on as you scroll — all your albums, where the list stopped at 200 —, and past 1,000 entries Show more lists the next ones.

Edit a radio station from Favorites. Edit station now opens its form in Favorites too, where the tap showed nothing — and the form comes into sight wherever the station sat in the list.

Installation

The app on your phone, without guessing. The manual's First run takes you through it device by device — iPhone or iPad, Android, Mac, Windows —, one action per step, in the words your phone shows, down to the Open as Web App switch that makes the icon an app on an iPhone. The installer's last screen gives the same steps.

When the page does not open. The manual says what refused to connect means, and how to read the box's address, name and port on the box itself; and how to find the box's name again when the installer's last screen is gone.

Outputs

Your DAC stays your DAC. Each service now names the sound card it plays on instead of using its number, which could change from one start of the box to the next: the music no longer moves to another card, and the DAC no longer disappears after a restart. The update converts your existing settings only where it can confirm which card they meant; any other keeps working as before, and is converted the next time you choose that output or run the guided setup.

The output switch where it works. The UPnP renderer and Roon Bridge get their output pills on the phone; the NAA, whose output is chosen in HQPlayer, no longer offers a switch, and says so if asked. Spotify Connect, which Audiogravity never played, leaves the signal path of the boxes set up before July.

When a track does not play, the message says why. Track could not be reached or Track could not be read, where the app said Output unavailable though the output was fine.

The diagram's controllers light when they play. With Controllers on, a phone app such as JPlay lights only while it really plays through the box — no longer whenever the box plays.

Streaming

A track a service cannot provide no longer stops the music. When Qobuz, Tidal or HIGHRESAUDIO cannot provide a track — which happens even with a subscription —, the next one plays and a message names the one skipped; when none of them can be had, the message says so, in words. The app no longer shows the box's internal addresses, nor its key.

Signing in to Tidal, without a button — and in Chrome or Edge on a computer, without a paste. Pasting Tidal's return address into its card completes the sign-in: the Finish login button is gone. In Chrome or Edge on a computer, on the box's https address, coming back to the app is enough: once the browser is allowed to share the clipboard, the card picks up Tidal's address — and nothing else. A closed or blocked Tidal page, or a refused address, no longer means reloading: Reopen Tidal page starts over.

Audio Software

An update leaves your services as they were. A service that was running runs again once its update is over, on the new version — MPD used to stay stopped after its own update. One you stopped, or set to DISABLED, stays so, even when its package would turn it back on; and what you change on the Services page or with a profile while the update downloads is what you keep. The confirmation says This restarts … if it is running, and REPAIR warns you too.

Restart required when it is needed, on the right service. The badge now shows only when a service could not be restarted after its update — no longer after every update —, restarts the right service — it answered not managed for AirPlay, HQPlayer NAA and Roon Bridge —, goes out by itself once the service has restarted, from the Services page or with the box, and every device shows the same.

UPnP Bridge on an ARM box moves to upmpdcli 1.10.1, its author's latest — mostly changes to the media server, according to the author: the Tidal and Subsonic plugins, icons for plugins, caching, and a crash fixed in OpenHome. The Audio Software tab offers it as an update: press CHECK UPDATES to see it at once, or let the box find it in its daily check. An x86 box already gets it from the author's repository.

Interface

The app opens in the snow at Christmas. On 24 and 25 December, by your device's clock, the installed app opens on a Christmas screen: snow falling on its black ground, Santa's sleigh across the sky, a tree whose lights blink beside the icon with two gifts at its foot, red and gold waves — Your audio chain, merrily controlled. The sign-in page opens the same way. The screen stays no longer than on any other day, nothing moves if your device asks for less motion, and the rest of the app does not change.

One picture for a missing cover. A track, an album or a playlist without artwork shows a music note, everywhere in the app; on a media server's lists, Play and + no longer touch. The full-screen player's Queue button now looks like the Queue tab.

On audiogravity.app

The landing shows the app as it is today. Six screens of its carousel are captured anew — the players, Stats, Tuning —, a new one shows the VU meters in place of the cover, and a new card shows the player on a phone held sideways, with its meters, and the showcase.

The press release, in English and in French. Audiogravity 1.0's press release — the release, a fact sheet and nine screenshots — is on the site, presented by a blog post and linked from the landing's footer, Press release EN / FR.

Christmas on the landing, and a gift to find. On 24 and 25 December, by your own clock, audiogravity.app dresses up for Christmas — a cartoon scene in the snow at the foot of the phone, falling snow, baubles, holly and Santa's sleigh — and the strip that shows a track playing plays the Nutcracker, with a button to hear an excerpt. On 24 December 2026, five lifetime licences hide on the page as gold gifts, one at a time through the day: the first visitor to come after one appears has twenty minutes to open it. The rules are on the site.

1.0.0 — 2026-11-06

Audiogravity 1.0 leaves the release candidates behind. Here is what it does, area by area; what changed since 1.0.0-rc.4 comes after, and the notes of every earlier version follow, for a box that comes from one of them.

Listening

One player for everything the box plays. The music on the box, internet radio, Qobuz, Tidal, HIGHRESAUDIO, your UPnP media servers, and AirPlay from an iPhone, iPad or Mac all play through the same controls, with their cover and the format of what plays — codec, sample rate, bit depth, bitrate. You queue from the library; the full-screen player has a sleep timer, fits a phone held sideways and, with Pro, swaps its cover for two VU meters that follow what you hear. Everything runs in a browser, and installs on a phone or a tablet as an app.

Your music

Your library, your services, your stations. The box plays your music from a NAS or a USB drive, and its Refresh reads the albums you add. With Pro, it browses Qobuz, Tidal and HIGHRESAUDIO — favourites, playlists, and your Qobuz and HIGHRESAUDIO purchases —, your UPnP media servers, and Roon's zones. Internet radio is a search away, with a filter for the stations that stream lossless. With Pro too, Stats tells what the box played: how long, from where, in what sound quality, which genres, and your most played.

Outputs and engines

The engines you trust, installed and driven for you. MPD plays to your DAC, DSD included — native or DoP —, and the box holds the DAC's own volume at maximum while DSD plays. A network renderer — a Marantz, a Linn, a DLNA speaker — can play in its place. With Pro, HQPlayer takes the music with its filters and noise shaping, as the desktop application, HQPlayer Embedded or the NAA. The box installs, configures and starts each engine itself: no configuration file, no terminal.

Setup and tuning

Set up in a few taps, tuned for listening. A guided setup finds your DAC and your music and writes the configurations for you; profiles switch between your listening setups in one tap. With Pro, the box tunes Linux for playback — real-time priorities and CPU cores kept for the audio services, CPU governors —, measures its own latency, and draws your whole signal path live, from the source to the DAC.

Running the box

A box you run from your phone. Accounts sign in with a password or a passkey, the box sends notifications to your phone, the configuration you edit is backed up first, and updates install from the app in one tap. It installs with one command on Debian 13 — DietPi and Raspberry Pi OS included —, on x86-64 or ARM.

Since 1.0.0-rc.4

Stats counts AirPlay and the radio in the right sound quality. AirPlay showed as Hi-Res: the box took the 32 bits its sound card is opened at for the music's, where the classic AirPlay it runs only ever sends 16 bit / 44.1 kHz. AirPlay now counts in the quality the iPhone, iPad or Mac sends — CD quality, here. A station started straight after another track, France Inter among them, could count as Unknown for its whole listen: its format came a moment after it started, and reached Stats only while the app was open. Listens already recorded keep their category, and the manual now says what Unknown means under Sound quality.

On audiogravity.app

The blog, rewritten around how the equipment sounds. The posts on the Naim NAIT 2, the BBC LS3/5A and the Quad 33 and 303 now open on why each became a reference, and give most of their room to how it sounds — as Stereophile, The Absolute Sound, Hi-Fi News and Hi-Fi World heard it, from its first reviews to today, where they agree and where they don't —, before its rivals, what to pair it with, and its history and figures. A new post on the Rega Planar 3 follows the same lines, down to a P3 of about 2005 playing through a NAIT 2 into a pair of LS3/5A.

1.0.0-rc.4 — 2026-11-02

Player

A level meter, needles and all. In the full-screen player, the switch below the cover swaps it for two needle dials that follow the music, drawn in your theme's own ink. On a tablet, Showcase mode opens them over the whole screen, side by side, with what plays below — the screen stays on, for a box on a shelf. The needles move with the sound you hear: the box waits until your DAC has played each moment before showing it, whatever the format, DSD included. They follow what the box's own player plays — the music on the box, the radio, Qobuz, Tidal and HIGHRESAUDIO —, not AirPlay, Roon, HQPlayer or a network speaker, and nothing runs for them while no screen shows them. They hide no problem: if another player holds your DAC, Play still tells you so. With Pro.

The full-screen player fits a phone held sideways. Turn your phone and everything shows at once, as on a tablet: the cover — or the level meter — on the left, the track, the controls and the output on the right, nothing to scroll. The showcase fits the screen too.

The progress bar keeps up with the music. After Next, the full-screen player's bar starts the new track at 0:00 — with Roon, AirPlay or a network speaker it carried on from where the previous track had stopped. Both bars, in the full-screen player and the mini-player, now follow a track that goes back: restarted, moved back from your phone or another app, or followed by another of the same name. A track played again with Previous starts from its beginning.

AirPlay: the right cover, and no seek it cannot make. Each AirPlay track shows its own cover; on a change of track, the previous one could stay in its place. And the bar of an AirPlay track no longer offers to move within it: AirPlay cannot, and the box refused every move.

On audiogravity.app

A place to talk with other owners. The site now has a community, on GitHub Discussions: ask a question and mark the reply that solves it, suggest what Audiogravity should do next, show your system, and follow the announcement of each release. The landing links it as Community, at the bottom of the page, and its roadmap now asks you to say which direction matters most to you in the community's Ideas; in the manual, Getting help sends questions there. Bug reports still go to the issues.

Easier to find your way in the guides, the blog and the manual. The guides are filed under four themes — getting the DAC to work, your music and the network, streaming services and players, signal and volume — and each one opens on its short answer, in a box, with what it was checked against and when just under its title. A long page lists its parts beside the text, the one you are reading marked as you scroll — under the title, on a phone —, and every page ends with the previous and the next one. The blog's front page shows each post as a card with its photograph, and the top bar takes you from the guides to the blog and back.

Better shared, easier to find. A guide, a post or a chapter of the manual pasted into a forum or a message now shows a proper preview — a post with its photograph —, and search engines are told what each page is, when it was last checked and where it sits in the site.

HIGHRESAUDIO, Qobuz or Tidal? A new blog post compares the three services Audiogravity plays: who runs them and from where, the size of their catalogues, what they stream and what they sell, what they cost, how you listen from your phone to your hi-fi, what they say they pay artists, what they do about music made by AI, and which suits what — with what Audiogravity does with each, at the end.

1.0.0-rc.3 — 2026-10-31

Library

Refresh finds the music you just added. Copy albums onto your NAS, press Refresh in the Library — at the end of the bar or beside the Albums title — and the box reads your music folder again: the icon turns, what is already listed stays on screen, then the new albums appear. Your music keeps playing meanwhile. If the NAS is off or unreachable, the box leaves your library as it is and tells you, rather than emptying it. Streaming sources refresh as before.

Covers appear sooner, and weigh less on the player. The player handed a cover over in small pieces, reading its file again for each one — half a second for a large cover stored inside a music file. It now hands it over in one or a few large pieces: about a hundredth of a second, and the player's processor stays with the music while you browse. Covers saved beside your music, as a cover.jpg, come faster too.

Performance

Your music first, for real. MPD gives the threads that play your music real-time priority on its own. The Audio Optimized preset used to put all of MPD above them: reading album covers while music played could then hold the sound back — long enough to make it drop out on a box whose MPD buffer had been shortened by hand. On MPD the preset now leaves that to MPD, the editor warns if MPD is set above its own sound, and the real-time monitor shows MPD as real time — and warns if anything outranks its sound. The editor also opens on the service's own settings, no longer on a value typed and then cancelled. If you applied Audio Optimized to MPD: in Systemd tuning, open MPD, click Audio Optimized again, then Save Changes.

Notifications

A number on the app's icon. On an iPhone or iPad, the icon of the app on your Home Screen now shows how many notifications the box has sent since you last opened the app — opening it clears the number. A notification that arrives while the app is open does not count, and each device keeps its own number. Rather do without? Turn Badges off for Audiogravity in your device's notification settings.

On audiogravity.app

A blog. Beside the manual and the guides, the site has a Blog tab: dated articles on hi-fi equipment, and on using it with a streamer today. The first ones tell the story of Naim's NAIT 2 and the amplifiers that followed it, how to tell the year a Naim was made from its serial number, the Heed Abacus DAC — and why its DSD stops when its USB volume is lowered —, the BBC's LS3/5A loudspeaker, and Quad's 33 and 303, its first transistor amplifiers — and which of the 33's inputs a DAC belongs on —; a new guide goes with them: connecting a streamer to an amplifier's round DIN sockets.

1.0.0-rc.2 — 2026-10-27

Player

The fullscreen player stays put when you swap a station's covers. Tapping the small cover in the corner of a station's artwork, to swap the station's logo and the track's cover, made the whole player drop off the screen and slide back up; a new track could do the same once you had touched the screen. It now stays in place. With a mouse, Close slides the player down instead of making it vanish, and a swipe that iOS interrupts — a call, Control Center — puts the player back in place.

Library

Stats in the latency histogram's colours, in Minimal. In the Minimal theme, the Stats view's sound quality and listening hours use the four colours of the latency test's histogram: DSD in grey, Hi-Res in black, CD in amber and MP3/AAC in red, and the hours from grey for the quietest to red for the busiest. Slate and Gravity look as before.

Interface

Messages you can close, that stay until they are read. The messages that say how an action went have an × now. An error or a warning stays until you close it; a success goes away after a few seconds, unless your pointer or your finger rests on it. Three show at most, never the same one twice, and an action that takes a while turns its "in progress" message into its result instead of stacking a second one. They no longer cover the top bar of the app installed on an iPhone, the tabs, or the offline banner's RETRY, and on a phone they are centred. Their words were reviewed too: each title says the outcome, and a favourite that cannot be saved says why, where a blue "ERROR" said nothing.

Your whole library plays through HQPlayer, whatever it is encoded in

HQPlayer reads a short list of formats, and an Apple-encoded library is not on it. Hand it an ALAC file and it answers with an error without so much as opening it — served over the network or given by its path, it makes no difference. That mattered more than it sounds: a library ripped with iTunes or Apple Music is almost entirely M4A, and the media server on our own network answers with 185 M4A files out of 200. Choosing HQPlayer as your output meant losing nearly all of your music, and the only advice we could give was to convert the lot yourself, with something else, and keep two copies.

You no longer have to. Audiogravity converts each track to FLAC as it hands it over. Losslessly — nothing is lost that was not already lost — and your files are never touched: what is converted is the copy going out, and it is thrown away afterwards. A CD-rate track takes about two seconds and comes out the same size as the original.

And you can still move inside a track. That is the part worth insisting on, because it is what a simple relay would have cost you. The conversion is a complete file rather than a stream, so it is served with its length and can be read from any point: ask for three minutes in and HQPlayer goes there. A converted album behaves like any other.

This covers three paths at once. A file in your own library. A track a media server publishes — until now the same album was accepted from the box and refused from the NAS, which took some explaining. Audiogravity now relays those as they arrive, for well under one per cent of a processor core.

Both were found the same way — by watching what the box actually publishes while someone moves a slider, rather than by reasoning about the code — and both turned out to be different from what they looked like.

A DSD album could leave every volume at maximum. DSD is a 1-bit stream, and any attenuation degrades it, so Audiogravity raises the volumes to 100 while one plays and puts them back when it ends. That is why the slider is replaced by a padlock during DSD: the level is deliberately out of your hands for the duration.

The putting-back only happened if something was still playing at that precise moment. When a DSD album simply reached its end — or was stopped before choosing what came next — the restore was skipped, and the levels being held were thrown away in the same breath. Nothing later could recover them. Whatever you played next played at 100, and the only way back was to set the volume by hand. It looked intermittent because it depended on something else happening to be paused at the right instant.

Restoring now happens whether or not the music is still running. One subtlety shaped the fix: a player that fails to answer is indistinguishable, from the outside, from a player that has stopped — and writing a level on the first case would attenuate a stream still in flight, the exact harm the whole mechanism exists to prevent. So the end of a DSD stream is now established by asking, once, instead of inferred from silence. When the answer does not come, nothing is written and nothing is forgotten.

The slider was covering that with a fixed pause of a second and a half. Measured against a running box, the correct value arrived after 1.26 s — a quarter of a second of margin. Every transport control in Audiogravity — play/pause, next, previous, volume, repeat, shuffle — used to work on trust. The command was sent to the player, the interface flipped at once, and the player's answer was never read. Almost always that trust was well placed, which is why it went unnoticed for so long. The exceptions were quietly corrosive: a DAC with no volume control of its own refuses every volume change, and the slider moved anyway; a player mid-restart drops commands on the floor, and the pause button toggled over music that never stopped.

The seek was converted first — it is described below, and it forced the question of what the other six controls should do when the player says no. The answer, chosen deliberately: say nothing, and be right. On a refusal the button or slider simply returns to the true state. No toast, no error banner — a control that visibly does not act is the honest message, and a notification would be noise for something as rare as an output without a mixer. The volume panel was the last holdout: it kept showing the level you dragged to until you closed it. It now glides back to the real level a moment after you let go — invisible when the change took, honest when it did not.

Two things stand behind this, worth a sentence each. After every command, refused or not, the player broadcasts its actual state, and that broadcast — not the interface's optimism — is the reference every screen converges on. And switching between audio outputs was found capable of something worse than lying: it could disable every output and report success. The new output is now switched on before the old ones are touched, so a refusal leaves the music playing where it was, and the refusal reaches you with the player's own reason.

The same treatment then went to everything that adds music rather than steers it: queueing a track or an album, starting a radio station, removing a row from the queue. All of them answered "done" without reading the player's reply, and the failures they hid are the ordinary kind — a station whose address the player rejects, an album that left the library index since the page was drawn, a queue row someone already removed. You would click, see the interface agree, and hear nothing. Now each says what happened, in the player's own words.

Two refusals are deliberately not passed on, because failing the whole request would lie in the other direction. When you queue an album and the player stops partway through the list, the tracks that made it in are yours — they are kept, and counted honestly. Tidal delivers its lossless tracks in pieces rather than as a file, so Audiogravity reassembles each one on the fly and hands it to the player as it is being written — which is why playback starts about a second after you press play instead of after a full download. The self-update safety net, made real

Audiogravity can update itself, and an update that fails is supposed to undo itself and leave the box exactly as it was. Tested on real hardware — by deliberately breaking an update — it did not. The undo restored the core program and nothing else, then wrote previous version restored in its log. Everything else an update rewrites was left as the failed version: the service definition, the package registry, the privileged helpers — and the update mechanism itself. A bad update could leave a box that could no longer update, while its own log said all was well.

The undo now restores everything the update overwrote — measured, nine files — reloads the service definition, and tells the truth: if any part cannot be put back, it says so rather than claiming success. And after undoing a failed update, the box can update again, which is the whole point.

Fixing this surfaced a second, sharper problem in the same machinery. The privileged helper that carries out an update runs with administrator rights, and it used to take the address it downloaded-and-ran, and the path it wrote, from whoever asked. Anything already running under Audiogravity's own limited account could hand it a malicious address and have it run as administrator — a local escalation to full control of the box. The helper now owns those values and refuses to take them from its caller; it accepts only a checked list of the rest. Proven on real hardware: an injected address is dropped and never runs.

But the limit has nothing to do with radio: an ALAC album sitting in your own library failed exactly the same way — HQPlayer accepted the request, then never started, and several seconds later Audiogravity blamed the sound card. The wrong culprit, after a wait.

The check now sits on the single path every source goes through, so it holds for your library, your media server and radio alike. The answer is immediate and names the format, and an album is checked before anything is sent — one unplayable track is caught up front rather than stopping the music halfway through. Formats Audiogravity cannot identify are still attempted: only what HQPlayer is documented not to handle is refused.

An album is treated as a whole, deliberately. A record where a single bonus track is ALAC is refused entirely rather than played without it: an album missing a track you never noticed was dropped is a worse answer than a clear refusal naming the track.

One case remains unguarded, deliberately: some media servers publish tracks at addresses that reveal nothing about the format. There, Audiogravity has nothing to go on and lets the attempt through rather than refusing on a guess.

0.9.5 — 2026-06-22

Core — Audio reliability & under-the-hood fixes

Two core modules (audio_pipeline and audio_hw) went through a thorough code review. The fixes are transparent to the end user but protect audio quality under load:

  • Event loop no longer blocked — all filesystem access in audio_hw and audio_pipeline is now off the event loop (asyncio.to_thread). On the Pi, a scan could stall for 50–200 ms, delaying SSE heartbeats and potentially causing audio glitches.
  • Accurate ALSA subdevice availability — GET /audio-hw/devices now reflects the real occupation state of ALSA devices (read from /proc/asound). Previously subdevices_available was always 1 even when MPD or HQPlayer held the device exclusively.
  • ?force_refresh=true — new query parameter on GET /audio-hw/devices to force an immediate rescan after a USB hotplug event, without waiting for the 60 s cache to expire.
  • Cache not corrupted on I/O error — a transient error during a scan (hotplug race, permission) no longer caches an empty or partial list; the next call retries cleanly.
  • Pipeline metrics corrected — HQPlayer volume clamped to [0, 100], cpu_percent() initialised correctly, ALSA latency accurate on ARM64 (64-bit wraparound).
  • HQPlayer "ghost track" in mini player fixed — after stopping HQPlayer completely (no track loaded), the mini player kept displaying the last played track indefinitely. The now-playing cache is now explicitly cleared when HQPlayer confirms it has nothing to play, while transient stops (DSP transitions, buffering) still show the last known state.
  • Config backup restore now works — restore_backup was silently rejected by sudoers in production (missing cp rule); backups can now be restored from the UI.
  • Backup files are now private — backup files (which may contain service passwords) are now correctly set to mode 600; previously the chmod silently failed and files were world-readable.
  • Config editor save/reload no longer stalls — all blocking I/O in the config service (file reads, sudo commands) now runs off the event loop, preventing audio glitches during a config save or package install.
  • Config validation no longer freezes the server — POST /config_validation/validate previously ran a systemctl subprocess per service synchronously on the event loop (up to N×5 s); checks are now parallel and non-blocking.
  • Login timing hardened — disabled accounts and nonexistent accounts now take the same response time, preventing username enumeration by measuring login latency.
  • Passkey registration and login no longer interfere — starting a passkey registration and a passkey login simultaneously for the same account no longer causes both flows to fail.
  • HQPlayer playback now works — every play_uri and play_library_item call was silently failing after loading the queue because the <Play/> command closes the connection without responding; the batch transport now handles this correctly.
  • Trial period survives a power loss — the trial file is now written atomically; a crash mid-write no longer produces corrupted JSON that is misdiagnosed as tampering and locks the user out of their trial.
  • License gate no longer bypassed at startup — if the license service fails to initialise, protected endpoints now return HTTP 503 instead of silently allowing all access.
  • Radio custom stations now actually work — a missing await made POST /radio/library/custom always fail with a serialisation error since the feature was introduced.
  • Tidal login errors are now reported correctly — previously the callback endpoint returned HTTP 200 even when the token exchange failed; it now returns HTTP 400 with a clear error message.
  • Server shutdown no longer hangs — SSE monitoring loops were never cancelled on shutdown due to a type mismatch (List[Task] iterated as a single Task); fixed so graceful restart is reliable.
  • Reduced CPU/memory pressure on Pi under load — several long-running blocking operations (audio ALSA scan, thermal zone reads, governor writes, stddev histogram expansion, os.fsync) are now off the event loop, reducing audio dropout risk and RAM pressure under sustained load.
  • License server XSS fixed — a crafted license key or server-controlled filename could inject HTML into the portal activation pages; the admin session token could be exfiltrated if the admin panel was open in the same browser session.
  • License resend and transfer now preserve version scope — resending or transferring a v1-scoped license was silently upgrading it to an all-versions license; upgrade paywall is now enforced correctly.
  • All-versions lifetime licenses no longer falsely rejected on AG v2 — licenses issued before version scoping was introduced are now correctly accepted on all AG versions.
  • UI XSS vulnerabilities fixed — three injection points in the license status and package update UI now HTML-escape or validate server-controlled values before rendering. A crafted core response could previously exfiltrate the admin JWT token.
  • UI no longer crashes on corrupted browser storage — auth initialisation handles a malformed jwt_user in localStorage gracefully instead of throwing an uncaught SyntaxError that left the app blank.
  • Config editor no longer accumulates memory — opening and closing the config editor multiple times no longer leaks CodeMirror instances; a disconnectedCallback now properly cleans up.
