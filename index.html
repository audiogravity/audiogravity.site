Internet radio without a parallel signal path

Most apps treat radio as a second-class source — a different player, a degraded path to the DAC, no metadata, no cover art. Here it isn't. Curate your own collection, filter the catalogue down to the stations that really stream lossless, save the ones worth coming back to. PRO

HQPlayer — on the box, or on your network

Run HQPlayer Embedded on the box itself: install it from the Audio Software page — its licence is shown first, and nothing installs until you accept it — choose its output as you would any player's, and open its own settings page in one tap. Or control the HQPlayer you already run on your network, auto-discovered and connected in one tap. Either way, change its sound-shaping settings — interpolation filter, noise shaper, output mode and volume — without leaving the couch, and send anything you can browse — your own library, a media server, internet radio, Qobuz, Tidal and HIGHRESAUDIO — through its filters and upsampling, bit-perfect. Run more than one? Each keeps the filter, shaper and mode you chose for it. Audio priority · Pro

End the jitter.

A 200 µs interrupt is enough to make a DSD256 stream audibly grainy. Audiogravity gives each audio service the priority it needs, and writes those settings beside the originals rather than over them — an update cannot undo your work, and one click puts everything back. Then measure the result yourself, in microseconds.

What you control, per service

  • SchedulingAudio threads run ahead of everything else — strict or round-robin. (SCHED_FIFO / SCHED_RR)
  • PriorityWho gets the CPU first, service by service — your music ahead of the rest. (1 → 99)
  • Dedicated coresEach service runs on the CPU cores you choose for it. (affinity)
  • MemoryAudio stays in RAM — never pushed to disk mid-track. (MEMLOCK)
  • Internal threadsA ceiling so a single service cannot starve the others. (RTPRIO)
  • CPU speedFull speed on the cores playing music, low power on the idle ones — re-applied at boot. (governor)

The takeaway: Audiogravity owns the interface and orchestration layers, installs, configures and drives the audio engines audiophiles already trust (MPD, upmpdcli, AirPlay, HQPlayer Embedded, the HQPlayer NAA + DSP, Roon, network UPnP renderers), connects the content sources they rely on (NAS, Qobuz, Tidal, HIGHRESAUDIO, internet radio, MinimServer), and tunes the OS kernel underneath — without replacing or hiding any of them.

Board and software

A streamer is a board
and a software layer.

Network streamers sell for anywhere between a few hundred and several tens of thousands of euros. For the part that makes one a streamer — receiving, decoding, handing the result to a DAC — it is a board running Linux with a software layer on top. That layer is, most of the time, the one Audiogravity drives: ALSA, MPD, upmpdcli, Shairport-Sync.

  • Check it yourselfThose components are licensed under the GPL, which obliges the manufacturer to declare them. The “open source licences” page in your machine's manual or web interface lists them.
  • Yours to chooseNothing here has to be built: a Raspberry Pi, a fanless mini-PC, an Intel Mac mini with Linux on it — a DAC you like, and you have a streamer. The DAC, the power supply, the clocks are simply not Audiogravity's ground — they are yours. A finished box hands you the whole chain as one decision; this way you change one stage at a time, and only when you want to.
  • Where a box agesThe software is usually where a finished machine is weakest — a closed application, no Roon, no HQPlayer, rarely all three streaming services — and it stops evolving the day the model leaves the catalogue.
  • Where this one doesn'tMPD is over twenty years old, ALSA close to thirty, and both are still developed today. Your machine will not be abandoned because a manufacturer changed its range.
