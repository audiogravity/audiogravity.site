The Naim NAIT 2: the small amplifier that became a reference

31 October 2026 · 10 min read

Checked on 2026-11-02 against the reviews of Stereophile (1989, 1990, 2016), Hi-Fi World (2007) and The Absolute Sound (2018), and Naim's owner's manual.

The NAIT 2 is the integrated amplifier Naim Audio made from 1988 to 1993: a box the size of a shoebox, a few watts, no remote, no headphone socket. From its first reviews to the latest, the specialist press has treated it, and the NAITs around it, as a reference among affordable integrated amplifiers. Hi-Fi World called the NAIT the "quintessentially British affordable audiophile amplifier"; Robert Harley, who first heard the NAIT 2 for Stereophile in 1989, wrote in 2018 that no other product "has brought more musical pleasure to more listeners for less money". In 2016, Stereophile listened to a rebuilt one again. Here is what they heard, where they disagree, how it compares with its rivals and its successors, and what to pair it with.

Why it became a reference

The first NAIT came out in 1983 at £253. It "became an instant classic", Hi-Fi World wrote in 2007: it "sounded quite unlike any other integrated of its day", and started the trend for "super integrateds", between entry-level amplifiers and pre-power combinations. For Harley, the cheap solid-state amplifiers of the time were "hard, bright, grainy, flat"; the NAIT "gushed glorious music".

It came from a firm with firm ideas. Art Dudley, in Stereophile, sums up its views of the time: "Shopping for amplifiers based on output power is foolish", and soundstaging was American "nonsense". When Sam Tellig asked Julian Vereker, Naim's founder, why the NAIT was so low-powered, Vereker told him a more powerful version had been tried while he was on holiday: "It sounded awful."

The NAIT 2 replaced it in 1988. Dudley credits its redesign to James Barrington-Brown, who gave it Naim's own quasi-complementary output stage and the firm's first two-sided circuit board; Hi-Fi World says it was voiced by Roy George, Naim's technical director. The reviews "poured in", Dudley writes, "and not from only the UK press". For Harley, "none of the Nait's enchantment was lost in the translation; the Nait 2 actually improved upon its legendary predecessor."

What the reviewers said of its standing:

  • Stereophile, 1989: "the most musically satisfying amplifier of the group", Harley wrote, after comparing it with three rivals.
  • Stereophile, 1990: Tellig could not "name a better-sounding integrated amp at the price".
  • Hi-Fi World, 2007: any NAIT, "new or old", would still be the reviewer's "top recommended integrated amplifier" between £500 and £900, and the old ones "brilliant second-hand buys".
  • Stereophile, 2016: Dudley put vintage Naim with the old products whose makers now do even better, "and yet the old product retains its hold on buyers' imaginations".
  • The Absolute Sound, 2018: the NAIT "may be" Naim's "most iconic product", and the NAIT line has won more of the magazine's Editors' Choice awards than any other product in its history.

The sound

Timing. Dudley found the NAIT 2's "uncanny way with musical timing" worthy of the praise Naim amplifiers get for it: "praise well deserved". Its momentum held even in slow music: in the Andante of Schubert's "Trout" Quintet, the system "didn't lose one iota of its momentum", and when the pace picked up, "my brain felt as if it were hot-wired to the musicians' intentions." Bass lines became legible: on Procol Harum's Home, Robin Trower's bass was "completely distinguished from the same notes played on the piano". Hi-Fi World, comparing four NAITs, called the first three "characterful, with a swaggering tunefulness" — "think of them as Keith Richards".

Voices and words. Next to the first NAIT, Hi-Fi World heard "obviously higher resolution to the vocals", a fuller, deeper sound, and the timbre of instruments "brilliantly". Dudley noticed the words: with the NAIT 2, "the words — and their meanings — simply leaped out", disc after disc.

Tone. Harley heard "no trace of edge or harshness": timbres "soft, warm, and liquid, very much like tube amplifiers", with "a slight loss of detail in the high end" that he preferred. Tellig found it "sweet, smooth, spacious", and said it sounded "a lot like a Krell" — "a 15Wpc Krell integrated amplifier... if D'Agostino dared to think small." Dudley, next to his valve amplifiers, heard textures "of a thicker, less stringy sort" — "little rounded nubs, not little spikes" — and a sound slightly brighter overall, "but not bright per se".

Bass. Hi-Fi World found it "wonderfully tuneful", the most tuneful of the four NAITs, over quieter backgrounds. Dudley heard it "far tighter, with more precise attacks and cleaner decays" than through his own amplifier. Harley, in 1989, did not: next to its rivals it lacked "weight and authority", and was "slightly mushy".

Space. Here the reviewers disagree most. Harley's first impression was of a soundstage "huge, open, three-dimensional, and transparent", the speakers tending "to disappear into the music"; Tellig found it "above all" spacious. Hi-Fi World heard "a slight lack of 'air'" next to the first NAIT. Dudley found its sense of scale "less than grand", and the NAIT 2 "definitely not an ideal product for listeners who crave being able to hear the air surrounding the players" — with an upside: "solid, not puffy".

Its limits. It does not play loud. Harley found it underpowered at moderate to high levels; Tellig said it could not do justice to the Spica Angeli in his main listening room: "Not enough ooomph."

They did not hear it in the same conditions. Harley and Tellig heard new units, in 1989 and 1990, Harley against three other amplifiers; Hi-Fi World heard a 1988 chrome-bumper unit against the other NAITs, from a Naim CD player into Kudos C1 standmounts; Dudley heard a 1990 NAIT 2 rebuilt with new transistors and capacitors, into Quad electrostatics, against his own electronics.

Against its rivals, and the NAITs that followed

In 1989, Harley compared it with the Arcam Alpha 2, the Creek 4140 and the Audiolab 8000A. For soundstaging and tonal balance, the NAIT 2 was "the clear winner"; for power and bass, it lost. "It is the amplifier I would choose for overall musical satisfaction if price were no consideration," he wrote, having noted its "relatively high price", $795, and pointed readers on a budget to cheaper pre- and power amplifier pairs.

In 2007, Hi-Fi World heard the NAIT, NAIT 2, NAIT 3 and NAIT 5i one after the other:

What Hi-Fi World heard
NAIT, 1983 The best musical timing, "precision tempos"
NAIT 2, 1988 The most tuneful bass, a much quieter background than the NAIT
NAIT 3, 1993 The clearest, the most free-flowing; treble "a touch sharp" on female voices
NAIT 5i, 2003 The most refined, and more immediate: "more of a Mick Jagger"

The reviewer was "torn between Naits 2 and 3", and found the test "debunked the myth about the first Nait being the best". For Harley, reviewing it for The Absolute Sound in 2008, the 5i was "the best-sounding Nait yet".

The NAIT 50, the small NAIT's return

The NAIT 50 at the High End show in Munich, in May 2023. Photo: What Hi-Fi?, from its news of 18 May 2023.

For its fiftieth anniversary in 2023, Naim brought back the first NAIT's looks in the NAIT 50: the same small chrome-bumper case, a moving-magnet phono stage and line inputs on 5-pin DIN sockets, 25 W per channel into 8 Ω and a headphone output. Naim made 1,973 of them, after the year it was founded.

What to pair it with

  • Speakers: small and reasonably efficient, "preferably in a small room", was Tellig's advice. The pairings the reviewers heard: Monitor Audio 7 standmounts in Tellig's living room, Kudos C1 standmounts at Hi-Fi World, Quad electrostatics at Dudley's — the memory of a NAIT 2 on Quad ESLs, he wrote, had brought him "to the brink of buying a used Nait 2". Here, a NAIT 2 drives a pair of Rogers LS3/5A: what they ask of it is in The BBC LS3/5A.
  • A turntable: its phono stage takes a moving-magnet cartridge, at 47 kΩ. Tellig played his records through it from a Rega RB300 arm; Dudley plugged an EMT TSD 15 pickup, which he usually runs through a step-up transformer, straight into it: apart from some bass weight, "it sounded great".
  • Speaker cable: Naim's manual asks for its own cable, at least 3.5 m a side and of equal lengths, and warns that some other types may damage the amplifier.
  • Warm-up: Dudley switched it on well before listening, knowing "the breed's tendency to sound its best only when fully warmed up".
  • A DAC: it goes into Aux, through a cable with a 5-pin DIN plug at the NAIT's end: A streamer into an amplifier with DIN inputs.

Buying and servicing one

  • Which look. Chrome bumper, from 1988 to 1989: a black aluminium case with shiny edges, as in the photograph above. Olive, from 1989: a black case and a green front panel. Naim told Hi-Fi World that the olive case brought "no performance improvement", contrary to the rumours.
  • Its age. Its serial number gives the year it was made: see How old is your Naim?.
  • Mains voltage. Each unit was built for one mains voltage; check it on a NAIT 2 bought in another country.
  • A pre-amplifier conversion. Naim could convert a NAIT 2 into a pre-amplifier for its NAP 90 or NAP 140 power amplifiers; a converted unit no longer drives speakers on its own.
  • A CD version. On some units, a line input takes the place of the phono stage behind the RCA sockets.
  • Servicing. After more than thirty years, its capacitors may need replacing. In 2007, Naim charged from about £145 to £250 for a full rebuild, Hi-Fi World wrote. In the United States in 2016, AV Options' standard rebuild cost $1,195: 14 transistors, the four output ones among them, 32 capacitors, three potentiometers, six resistors, the speaker sockets and the feet. For owners who do it themselves, hi-fi AF published a step-by-step guide in 2020; its site has closed, and the link opens a copy kept on this site.
  • Prices. In 2007, Hi-Fi World put a second-hand olive unit from £345 and a chrome-bumper one at about £445.

The facts

From Naim's manual, unless another source is named:

NAIT 2
Phono Moving magnet, RCA sockets: 2.5 mV, 47 kΩ
Tuner, Aux, Tape 5-pin DIN sockets: 75 mV, 47 kΩ
Loudspeakers 4 mm banana sockets
Size 205 × 76 × 276 mm
Power, Naim's figures 13 W into 8 Ω, 19.5 W into 4 Ω
Clipping, measured 21 W into 8 Ω, 33.5 W into 4 Ω
Price in the US, 1989 $795

Naim never printed a power figure in its manual, which speaks instead of "a very large peak current capacity". Its figures came out in Hi-Fi World in 2007; Stereophile measured where it clips in 1989. In between, the NAIT 2 was given 15 W, or 18 W: "depending on what day someone asks you", Tellig told Vereker. Inside, Harley found discrete transistors only, no op-amps, on a densely packed two-sided board.
