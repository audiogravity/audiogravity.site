Software or hardware volume on a Linux streamer: what each does to your music

Guide 14 · 4 min read

Short answer: you can turn the music down in three places. In the player, a software volume recalculates every sample, except at 100 %. In the DAC, a hardware volume leaves the samples as the player sent them, and the DAC lowers the level itself. On the amplifier, nothing digital is touched. For bit-perfect playback, keep the player's volume at 100 or off. With DSD, a player's software volume does nothing, and some DACs' own volume breaks it.

Measured on 2026-10-22 on Debian 13 with MPD 0.24.5 and a USB DAC built on an XMOS interface; the 16-bit and DSD cases on 2026-10-17 and 18.

The three places

  • The player's software volume multiplies every sample by a factor below one. The result is a new set of numbers: below 100 %, nothing reaches the DAC as it is in the file.
  • The DAC's hardware volume is a control the DAC offers to the computer. The player sends the samples untouched and asks the DAC to lower the level; how the DAC does it, inside its chip or in its analogue stage, is up to the DAC.
  • The amplifier comes after the DAC: the whole digital chain stays as it is.

Does your DAC have a volume of its own

amixer -c 0 scontrols
amixer -c 0 sget 'Heed Abacus ',0

With your DAC's card number, from aplay -l, in place of 0, and the name the first command prints in the second. For the DAC above:

Simple mixer control 'Heed Abacus ',0
Simple mixer control 'Heed Abacus ',1
  Limits: Playback 0 - 127
  Front Left: Playback 127 [100%] [0.00dB] [on]

It has two, from −127 dB to 0 dB in steps of 1 dB. A DAC that lists no control has no volume of its own that Linux can set.

In MPD

Each output of /etc/mpd.conf chooses its volume with mixer_type:

audio_output {
    type           "alsa"
    name           "USB DAC"
    device         "hw:0,0"
    mixer_type     "hardware"
    mixer_device   "hw:0"
    mixer_control  "Heed Abacus "
    mixer_index    "0"
}
  • hardware sets the DAC's own control. MPD looks for one named PCM unless told otherwise: copy the name amixer prints, between the quotes, spaces included, and its index when two share a name. A name MPD cannot find leaves the volume at n/a, and MPD's log says why: Failed to read mixer for "USB DAC": no such mixer control: PCM with no name given, …: Heed Abacus without the final space of the name above.
  • software computes the volume in MPD.
  • none leaves the level at full: set it on the amplifier.

By default, MPD uses the hardware volume of a device that has one, and none for the others.

What MPD's software volume does

  • The slider is not in decibels. Measured with a 1 kHz tone: 90 is −3.6 dB, 75 is −9.0 dB, 50 is −18.5 dB, 25 is −29.8 dB, 10 is −41.1 dB and 1 is −60.2 dB, against 100.
  • At 100, 24-bit audio passes untouched. 16-bit audio is widened to 24 bits, every value multiplied by 256: the same music in a longer word (see How to check bit-perfect playback).
  • Below 100, every sample is recalculated.

DSD and volume

  • MPD's software volume does nothing to DSD, even when MPD converts it to PCM for a DAC that takes no DSD: a DSD track came out the same at volume 100 and at 30.
  • A DAC's own volume may break DSD. The DAC above, its volume lowered by 10 dB, played DoP as silence and native DSD as noise. With such a DAC, leave its volume at maximum while it receives DSD, and set the level on the amplifier (see DSD on a Linux streamer).

With Audiogravity

Audiogravity's guided setup gives MPD its software volume: the player's slider is MPD's, and at 100 your music reaches the DAC as described above. While the DAC receives DSD, the slider gives way to a padlock, and the DAC's own volume goes to its maximum, then back; Full DAC volume during DSD, in MPD's Guided view, turns that off for a DAC that lowers DSD properly.

The details are in the manual: Listening.
