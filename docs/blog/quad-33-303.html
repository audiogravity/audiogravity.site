The Quad 33 and 303: Peter Walker's transistor classic, and its return

31 October 2026 · 9 min read

Checked on 2026-11-02 against the reviews of Hi-Fi News (1968 and 2011, through its 2025 sidebar, and 2025), Stereophile (2025) and The Absolute Sound (2025), Quad's instruction books for the 33 and the 303, old and new, and the history of the firm Quad wrote in 1982.

In 1967 Quad went from valves to transistors with a pair: the 33 control unit, a compact pre-amplifier, and the 303, a slim power amplifier with no switch at all. It became "one of the best-selling pre/power amp combinations of all time", Hi-Fi News wrote in 2025: 120,000 33s sold up to 1982, 94,000 303s up to 1985. They were among the first solid-state separates, admired for their design, and voiced, in Ken Kessler's words, "first and foremost for the Quad 57 ESL". Originals are still sought after, and in 2024 Quad brought both names back. Here is what made the pair a classic, what is known of how the originals sound, what reviewers heard from the new ones, and how to use an original today.

A Quad 33 of 2024, a 303 behind it. Photo: Quad.

What made it a classic

Quad is the brand of the Acoustical Manufacturing Company, which Peter Walker founded in London in 1936. The name came in 1951, with an amplifier: QUAD, for Quality Unit Amplifier Domestic. Then came the Quad II valve amplifier, in 1953, and in 1957 the electrostatic loudspeaker known today as the ESL-57.

The 33 and the 303 followed in 1967, Quad's "first all-transistorised" units, in its own words of 1982. Martin Colloms, in Stereophile, calls them "highly original, elegantly styled", and "among the first solid state separates"; the 303 "may have been one of the first 'vertical' amplifiers".

The original 33 and 303. Photo: Quad.

  • The size. "Compactness was the 33's greatest trend-setting move," Kessler writes. The 303 has no controls, not even a mains switch: it runs from an outlet on the 33, whose volume knob turns the whole system on, and Quad suggested it could sit out of sight, in a cabinet.
  • The Triples. In the 1960s, Colloms explains, no PNP output transistor could be paired with an NPN one for low enough crossover distortion. Quad's answer, the output stage it called the Triples, used NPN power transistors only; Quad wrote that it also overcame the problem of keeping the output transistors' idle current steady as they warm up.
  • The design. In 1969 the 33, the 303 and the FM3 tuner won a design award from the Council of Industrial Design. Colloms describes products "much admired for their modern industrial design", "enduring, widely feted successes" until they were discontinued.
  • The price. A 33 and a 303 cost £98 in 1967, about £2,230 today, Hi-Fi News reckoned.

And they outlived their production: the 2024 versions were "apparently moved" by "the continuing hot market for originals", Colloms writes.

How the originals sound

Hi-Fi News reviewed the pair in April 1968 and tested "mint" originals again in July 2011. I could not read either review; the magazine summed up both sets of measurements in 2025. The most direct account I found of how an original pair sounds today is Kessler's, who played one beside the pair of 2024: "the 1960s pairing still sounds wonderfully warmer", but "lacks the ultimate precision, bass control and extension at the frequency extremes". Consider what Walker "unleashed in 1967", he adds, "and you accept that these were voiced first and foremost for the Quad 57 ESL — their most likely partner".

What the bench found, in 1968 and 2011:

  • The 33 had a gently falling treble, 0.4 dB down at 20 kHz, and a steep filter at the bottom of the audible range, 3 dB down at 20 Hz and 18 dB at 10 Hz; distortion from 0.014 to 0.075 %.
  • The 303 gave a full 50 W per channel into 8 ohms, more than Quad's 45, with distortion "impressively uniform at ~0.02%" through the upper bass and midrange. Its treble fell as the load dropped, from 0.55 dB down at 20 kHz into 8 ohms to 1.8 dB into 2 ohms, and in short bursts it gave only 10 W into 1 ohm: "rather less capable of driving low impedances" than the 303 of 2024, Paul Miller concluded.

Its controls shaped the sound too. Besides bass, treble and balance, the 33 has a filter against the surface noise of records, a slope knob with buttons at 5, 7 and 10 kHz, and a Cancel button that takes the tone controls and filter out of the circuit, as a reference.

The 33 and 303 of 2024

In 2024 Quad, now part of the IAG group, brought both names back: new circuits by engineers led by Peter Comeau, in cases close to the originals. Kessler finds them "exactly how one would imagine the 33/303 might have evolved had they never ceased production".

The 33 and the 303 of 2024. Image: Quad, from its QUAD 33 page.

What reviewers heard.

  • Hi-Fi News, January 2025. Kessler: "Forget everything you know about the sound of the original 33/303." The new pair keeps "the smoothness of the originals but with added detail", a "blessedly wide soundstage", and sounds "'huge' in the literal sense" on the big stereo showpieces of the originals' era. Its phono stage is "easily worth" a £500 to £700 phono amplifier. "Peter Walker would be proud. I am buying a set as my mid-priced reference."
  • Stereophile, June 2025. Colloms heard "an impressive neutrality — no fireworks but rather an easy refinement", "neutral, almost nostalgic", a little sweeter with the tone controls bypassed; and "the 303 comes to life in bridged configuration", with "tighter, more tuneful bass with better rhythmic patterning". His verdict: "a calm, almost relaxed musicality respectful of its legendary roots".
  • The Absolute Sound, June 2025. Chris Kelly, who had lived with a Quad 44 and 405, found the music had "real rhythmic drive"; with two 303s bridged, "the same music positively leapt" from his Harbeths. Next to modern gear, the Quads are "slightly less analytical, perhaps a tad warmer".

All three heard more from two 303s bridged, one per channel: in Kessler's words, "not just the available power" but "bass solidity, dynamic contrasts and speed".

What changed.

  • The 33 has no DIN sockets any more: three RCA line inputs and one XLR, a moving-magnet and moving-coil phono stage, a headphone socket, a remote control, a motorised volume control and a display. Among its tone controls is a tilt, which tips the response about 700 Hz; the original's manual lists bass, treble and the filter.
  • The 303 takes RCA or XLR, and gives 49.5 W into 8 ohms and 70 W into 4, by its manual; bridged, 140 W into 8 ohms or 170 W into 4. Its output stage is now fully complementary, on two supply rails, with no output capacitor.
  • The price: £1,199 each in the UK, by Hi-Fi News, and $1,599 each in the US, by Stereophile, which gives China as the place they are made.

Inside the 33 of 2024. Its toroidal transformer reads "Low noise transformer manufactured under the specifications of QUAD". Image: Quad, from its QUAD 33 page.

What to pair them with

  • The originals. The Quad ESL-57 above all, the partner Kessler says they were voiced for. Quad's manual asks for a small modification to ESLs older than serial number 16800, of March 1966. The 303 accepts speakers of 4 to 25 ohms, and Hi-Fi News' measurements show it less at ease as the impedance falls.
  • The new pair. Colloms advises "more efficient, higher-impedance speakers", even bridged. Kessler drove speakers of 4 to 15 ohms and 85 to 93 dB "and power was never an issue". Kelly's Harbeths and Wharfedale Super Lintons both suited them.

Using an original today

  • The 33's inputs are all on DIN sockets: a record player, two tuners, the second of which can be any other source at radio level, and a tape recorder. Two boards plug into its back and match its inputs to the equipment behind them: the Disc Adaptor, inserted by one of its four edges, for a low- or high-output magnetic cartridge, a ceramic one, or a spare position; the Tape Adaptor, with three levels for recording and three for replay, set by small screws on its underside.
  • A DAC goes into Tape Replay, set to its highest level, H: it expects 1 V and takes up to 10 V. Not into Radio, which expects 100 mV and takes 2 V at most, about what a DAC gives at full scale; and not into Disc, made for a cartridge's few millivolts. Quad's circuit diagram gives all the 33's inputs the same pins, left on 3, right on 5, ground on 2; Tape Replay shares its pins with Tape Record, so unplug any tape recorder from that socket. The cable, the pins and the volume are in A streamer into an amplifier with DIN inputs.
  • A 303 alone has no volume control: it needs a pre-amplifier. Its input takes the left channel on pin 1 and the right on pin 3, with ground on pin 2 — not the scheme of the 33's inputs.

The facts

From Quad's manuals for the originals:

Quad 33
Disc 2 or 5.6 mV magnetic, 100 mV ceramic
Radio 1 and 2 100 mV, 2 V at most; 100 kΩ
Tape replay 100 mV, 400 mV or 1 V, set on its board
Output 0.5 V, on a 4-pin DIN socket
Size, weight 26 × 9.2 × 16.5 cm, 3 kg
Quad 303
Input 4-pin DIN: 0.5 V, 22 kΩ
Power 45 W into 8 Ω, 28 W into 16 Ω
Loudspeakers 4 to 25 Ω
Output 0.3 Ω, through a 2,000 µF capacitor
Size, weight 12 × 15.9 × 32.4 cm, 8.2 kg

Each 33 input's level is what drives it to its rated output, 0.5 V; its maximum is what it takes before it overloads. The original 303's output stage runs from a single 67 V rail and reaches the speakers through that capacitor. Stereophile writes that "it's said" Quad sold 100,000 303s.
